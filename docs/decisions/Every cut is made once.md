# Every cut is made once

## Context

The jigsaw chapter (levels 21-25, 29 and 30) cuts a hand-drawn scene into
interlocking pieces on a grid, from 2x2 up to 4x3. The pieces then have to go
back together into the picture they came out of, in front of a two-year-old who
will happily press a piece into place and look at the result.

The way a jigsaw generator is usually written is per piece: for each cell of the
grid, walk its four sides and draw a tab on each internal one. The tab on the
right-hand side of a piece and the socket on the left-hand side of its neighbour
are then two separate curves, generated by two runs of the same arithmetic with
the sign flipped and the endpoints swapped.

Two curves that are meant to be the same curve is the whole problem. They agree
today because the arithmetic agrees; they stop agreeing the moment anything
about it changes - a rounded coordinate, a control point expressed relative to
the wrong end, a tab whose height is a share of a cell that is 119.99999 units
wide on one side of the line and 120.00001 on the other. What the child sees is
a hairline of background between two pieces that are both "in the right place",
or a piece overlapping its neighbour by a whisker. There is no test for
"neighbours look right" short of rendering the board and looking at it, and the
failure is a pixel wide.

This game already has an answer to exactly this question. An animal and the hole
it fills are drawn from **one** `#silhouette` path, used twice, which is why a
piece cannot drift out of alignment with its hole
([`product.instructions.md`](../../.github/instructions/product.instructions.md)).
A slice of an animal is the animal's own artwork through a clip path, so two
slices mesh along their shared edge because it *is* one edge
([Slices are clipped, not cut](<Slices are clipped, not cut.md>)).

## Decision

**Every internal cut is generated once, and handed to both the pieces it
divides.** `src/jigsaw.ts` builds two tables before it builds any piece: the
horizontal cuts and the vertical cuts. A piece is then assembled out of four
entries from those tables - two of them walked backwards, because a piece goes
round clockwise and two of its sides were minted in the other direction.

Walking a cut backwards is `reverseEdge`, and it is four lines: it reorders the
points of the cubics and recomputes none of them. So the piece below a cut draws
the very points the piece above it drew, in the other order. Not "to within a
rounding error" - the same numbers. A tab is a socket seen from the other side
because there is only one curve, exactly as a hole is a piece seen from the
other side because there is only one path.

**Cutting is clipping, not intersecting.** A piece is the scene's own markup
inside a `<g clip-path>` made from that outline. No artwork is intersected with
anything, so one hand-drawn scene serves a 2x2 board and a 4x3 board without
being redrawn, and two neighbours cannot draw the same pixel differently.

**The property is tested directly.** `tests/jigsaw.test.ts` asserts that a
piece's shared edge, reversed, is *deeply equal* to its neighbour's - at every
grid the table cuts at - and adds up the areas of every flattened outline to
show the pieces tile the picture box exactly. Neither test needs a browser and
neither is a judgement about how something looks.

## Consequence

The cutter is not free to make a piece in isolation. Anything that wants to
change one edge - a fancier tab, a tab that leans, a cut that wanders - changes
the entry in the table, and both neighbours follow. That is the point; a change
that cannot be expressed that way is a change that breaks the guarantee.

Which way each tab points is not free either, and for a different reason. The
tray packs every piece into a cell as big as the biggest piece draws, so a piece
with tabs on all four sides drags the whole board's scale down, and a piece with
no tab at all is the smallest thing on the board. So the directions are chosen
with one turning point per row and per column: no piece carries two tabs on one
axis, and none carries none. On the busiest board in the game - twelve pieces
across a landscape screen - that is the difference between the smallest piece
drawing eight per cent of the canvas and drawing nine and a half.

The generated outline is a piece's `outline`, so the guide under the frame draws
each cell from the same path the piece is clipped from, and a placed piece
covers its own line exactly.

Nothing here knows about the irregular partitions the shatter chapter wants. A
shatter piece is a cell with a different sort of edge; if it is built the same
way - every cut minted once, every piece clipped through its own outline - it
inherits the same guarantee for free.
