# Every cut is made once

## Context

The jigsaw kind cuts a hand-drawn scene into interlocking pieces on a grid,
which then have to go back together into the picture they came from. The
usual way to generate a jigsaw grid is per piece: for each cell, walk its
sides and draw a tab on each internal one, so the tab on one piece's edge
and the matching socket on its neighbour are generated by two separate runs
of the same arithmetic with signs flipped and endpoints swapped.

Two curves that are meant to be the same curve is the problem: they agree
today because the arithmetic agrees, and stop agreeing the moment anything
about that arithmetic changes even slightly, such as a rounding difference
between two edges of a cell that is supposed to be the same width on both
sides of a shared line. What a child then sees is a hairline gap or overlap
between two pieces that are each "in the right place." There is no way to
test for this short of rendering the board and looking at it, and the
failure is a pixel wide.

This game already answers exactly this question elsewhere: an animal and
its hole are drawn from one shared outline, used twice, which is why a
piece cannot drift out of alignment with its hole (see `product.md`), and a
slice of an animal meshes with its neighbour along a shared edge because it
*is* one edge (see [Slices are clipped, not
cut](<Slices are clipped, not cut.md>)).

## Decision

**Every internal cut is generated once, and handed to both pieces it
divides.** The cutter builds its tables of cuts before it builds any piece,
and assembles each piece from entries in those tables - walking some of them
backwards, since a piece's boundary is traversed in one consistent direction
while a cut may have been generated by an earlier or later neighbour.

Walking a cut backwards only reorders its existing points; it recomputes
none of them. So the piece on one side of a cut draws the exact points the
piece on the other side drew, merely in the other order - a tab is a socket
seen from the other side because there is only one curve, the same way a
hole is a piece seen from the other side because there is only one path.

**Cutting is clipping, not intersecting.** A piece is the scene's own
artwork inside a clip made from that piece's outline. No artwork is
intersected with anything, so one hand-drawn scene serves any grid size
without being redrawn, and two neighbours cannot draw the same pixel
differently.

**The property is tested directly**, by asserting that a piece's shared
edge, reversed, is deeply equal to its neighbour's edge at every grid size
the cutter supports, and that the areas of every piece's flattened outline
sum to the picture's own area. Neither test needs a browser or a judgement
about how something looks.

## Consequence

The cutter is not free to build a piece in isolation: changing one edge -
a fancier tab, a different curve, a wandering cut - means changing the
shared table entry, and both neighbours change together. That is the point;
a change that cannot be expressed that way breaks the guarantee.

Which piece carries a tab on which side is also constrained, for tray
reasons: since every piece is packed into a cell sized to the largest piece,
a piece with tabs on every side would drag the whole board's scale down,
while a piece with none would be the smallest thing on the board. Tab
placement alternates along each row and column so no piece carries two tabs
on one axis and none carries zero.

The generated outline is also what a placed piece's guide is drawn from, so
a placed piece covers its own guide line exactly. Anything cut the same way
- every cut minted once, every piece clipped through its own outline -
inherits this guarantee for free, which is why the shatter kind's mesh
(see [Cut a picture into shards that are things to
hold](<Cut a picture into shards that are things to hold.md>)) reuses it
rather than reinventing it.
